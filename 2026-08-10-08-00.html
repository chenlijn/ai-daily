Method: FinEvo-Bench builds a longitudinal benchmark of 120 real-case-grounded tasks grouped into 20 business scenes across six finance domains. Conclusion: The paper gives agent teams a more realistic way to test whether memory, skill accumulation, and feedback loops actually compound over time in regulated work. Method: The authors cast dataset revision as utility-constrained realism improvement. Problem: Agents with large skill libraries cannot afford to stuff every tool description into context, but they still need to retrieve the right skills and sequence them coherently. The paper examines whether adding typed graph structure on top of retrieval actually helps, or whether a strong hybrid ranker already captures most of the reachable signal.

Method: The study compares two retrieval designs over 690 skills: a hybrid lexical-plus-dense ranker for sparse on-demand loading, and a typed knowledge graph that encodes prerequisites, data flow, and ordering relations. The evaluation uses 117 realistic non-echoing queries and tests the graph in its intended mode, where graph neighbors substitute for additional ranked results under the same token budget.

Results: The hybrid ranker retrieves the correct skill in the top five for 73.5% ± 8.0 of queries. The graph variant is 11.2 points worse at matched budget (p = 0.0007), 73% of ranker misses are not even reachable through the graph, and 98.6% of typed edges connect skills that the ranker had already surfaced together, which shows why the graph fails to extend recall.

Conclusion: The main lesson is refreshingly negative: extra structure is not automatically useful if it is built from the same neighborhood the retriever already searches. For agent-tool systems, better evaluation data and better candidate generation may matter more than adding an LLM-produced graph layer.

Problem: Standard RAG assumes chunking plus embedding search is enough, but that assumption breaks on long financial and regulatory documents filled with tables, repeated numbers, and distant headers. The paper targets cases where a wrong header or unit can silently change the meaning of a figure by orders of magnitude, making black-box top-k retrieval unsafe for audit-style work.

Method: The authors propose READ, an embedding-free agentic document search loop exposed through MCP. Instead of dense retrieval, the agent uses three deterministic operations, normalized lexical search, structural navigation, and bounded span reads, so every retrieval trajectory is inspectable and replayable as an audit trail.

Results: On 51 verified questions over a 780-page government financial report, READ answers 58.8% correctly versus 15.7% for dense retrieval, or 35.3% for a tuned dense baseline; READ still leads the tuned setup by 23.5 points with p_Holm = 0.017. A steelman table-aware chunker fixes some unit errors but still leaves 27 to 30% of numeric chunks without fiscal-year headers, and an agent with the same loop but only a top-k tool reaches just 27.5%.

Conclusion: This paper is important for enterprise document QA because it shows the retrieval interface can matter as much as the model. The practical boundary is also clear: the gain is against embedding-based retrieval, not necessarily against strong lexical search such as BM25, so teams should compare against simple transparent baselines before over-engineering agentic RAG.

Problem: Reasoning-model RL with verifiable rewards suffers from sparse sequence-level signals, and even on-policy self-distillation still treats local divergences too uniformly across a rollout. Method: DASH adds divergence-adaptive supervision horizons to on-policy self-distillation. Instead of weighting each local teacher-student discrepancy the same way, it maps the gap between each local signal and the sequence context into a horizon-sensitive weighting scheme, so supervision strength depends on how the divergence evolved over time during autoregressive generation.

Results: The abstract presents DASH as improving standard OPSD by better exploiting rollout temporal structure on reasoning tasks, but it does not expose the full benchmark table or precise deltas in the visible snippet. The concrete claim is that adaptive horizon weighting yields denser and better-targeted token-level supervision than vanilla self-distillation, which translates into stronger reasoning learning under RLVR-style training.

Conclusion: This is a post-training paper to watch because it pushes on the mechanics of how dense teacher signals should be scheduled, not just whether they exist. For teams tuning reasoning models, the implication is that rollout dynamics and credit assignment can be as important as choosing the teacher or reward source.
